Follow the money

When a budget says it funds something, does the money stay there? This page tracks three things in Washington's budgets since 2017: money moved between accounts, changes made after a budget first passed, and whether claims of a shortfall hold up. Every item shows the bill that made the decision and how the Legislature voted. The numbers come straight from the budget bills and official state reports; what they mean is up to you.

1. Money moved between accounts 2. Changes after a budget passed 3. Is there really a shortfall?

1. Where was money moved between accounts?

How to read this

The state keeps money in hundreds of accounts. Many are dedicated: set up by law for one purpose, like public works loans or toxics cleanup. Budget bills can move money out of them with lines like "For transfer to the state general fund, $X". Some transfers follow a formula in state law (marked required by law); others are choices made in that budget. "Up to" means the bill sets a maximum, not an exact amount. Moving money isn't wrong by itself. It's how budgets are balanced, so the point is to see where it happens, how often, and who voted for it.

2. What changed after the budget first passed?

How to read this

Washington passes a two-year budget in odd years, then changes it in "supplemental" budgets, usually twice. A program funded at one level when the budget passed can end up very different. This shows each agency or program's amount as first passed, as it stands now, and which later bill made the change. Increases often follow higher caseloads or costs; cuts can mean savings, delays, or money that wasn't going to be spent.

3. Is there really a shortfall, and what's driving it?

General Fund spending per resident, after inflation

How to read this

A claim that the state "needs more money" can be tested against two things it has to keep up with: more residents and rising prices. If spending per resident, adjusted for inflation, is flat, the budget is roughly keeping pace. If it's rising, the state is doing more, or paying more, per person than before. Figures cover the General Fund and three related accounts (NGF-O) that the Legislature must balance; they come from the official budget outlook adopted with each budget. Population and Seattle-area inflation come from the state's Economic and Revenue Forecast Council. Projected periods assume no new decisions (current law only), so they usually understate future spending: a projected drop is not a planned cut.

Data & methods

Every number on this site comes from a public Washington State or federal source, fetched and parsed by open-source code. Known gaps and judgment calls are documented, not hidden.

Important limits

  • Current-biennium spending isn't shown yet. OFM's budget-vs-actual reports are annual. The first 2025-27 report (fiscal year 2026) hadn't been published when this was built.
  • "Spent" is budgetary basis, which differs on purpose from the audited financial statements (ACFR).
  • Agency spending plans (allotments) and program-level spending aren't publicly downloadable, so they aren't shown.
  • Fund-source totals and statewide totals exclude transfers between funds to avoid double counting.
  • Party is shown as text only. Colors are never used to mark a party.
